Pentagon's First UFO Chief: Bosses Ordered the Answers Removed

By Jordan Vale · November 17, 2026 · 6 min read

Sean Kirkpatrick, the Pentagon's first UFO chief, says Pentagon leadership ordered his former office to cut the resolution language out of UFO case files and release those cases to the public as unresolved. That office was the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, known as AARO, and Kirkpatrick ran it from July 2022 to December 2023. On Fox News in November 2026, he said those cases had answers. He also said he had briefed several of those answers to Congress before they were cut.

This changes how every UFO file the Pentagon has released should be read. The man who built the office's case work is saying "unresolved" was assigned by leadership after the analysts had finished. The files the public got were edited down from records that already held conclusions.

Who Sean Kirkpatrick Is and Why His Word Carries Weight

Sean Kirkpatrick is a physicist who spent his career inside the defense intelligence system. Before AARO he was chief scientist at the Defense Intelligence Agency's Missile and Space Intelligence Center at Redstone Arsenal in Huntsville, Alabama, the unit that studies foreign missile and space threats.

In July 2022 the Pentagon named him the first director of AARO. Its job was to take every military UFO report and run it to ground. He held the post until December 2023.

His record stacks in threes. On April 19, 2023, he testified before the Senate Armed Services subcommittee on emerging threats. On May 31, 2023, he presented AARO's case work at NASA's public UAP panel. He also gave closed-door briefings to members of Congress on the cases his office had closed. He knows where the answers went because he held them.

What He Says the Bosses Ordered

On Fox News in November 2026, Kirkpatrick said Pentagon leadership told AARO to remove all of the resolution language and put the cases out as unresolved.

He says those cases had answers, and that he briefed several of them to Congress himself. The files that reached the public carried a different stamp.

Follow the sequence. An investigation reaches a finding. The finding goes to lawmakers behind closed doors. Then it is cut from the version the public reads, and the case is relabeled as open.

The War Department will not comment on any specific file. That refusal is a receipt in its own right. A department with a clean record could answer the charge in one sentence, and it chose silence.

The Office Built to Explain, Told to Stop Explaining

AARO had one job, and the word is in its name: resolution. Pilots reported objects, sensors logged tracks, and AARO's analysts were there to name what had been seen.

The office that existed to explain the sightings was told to stop explaining them.

Cut the resolution language and the office's product disappears. The work still happened and the analysts still reached conclusions. Those conclusions now survive in one place, the classified briefings Congress already received. The public holds the outer shell of each case, and a small group of cleared lawmakers holds what was inside it.

That makes two records. Cleared insiders get one version, and the taxpayers who fund the office get another. The same split runs through every era of buried findings in our Case Files.

The Boring-Answer Defense Breaks on Its Own Logic

The strongest pushback writes itself. Kirkpatrick left AARO with a reputation as a skeptic, so the pushback says his answers were balloons, drones, and sensor clutter, and that cutting them hid nothing worth seeing.

That argument falls apart once you ask what the cut gained anyone. Publishing a balloon answer costs nothing. It closes the case, ends the speculation, and makes the office look competent. Every incentive points toward printing it.

Leadership did the opposite. It took closed cases and reopened them on paper. An institution only pays that price when the answer itself is costly: a classified platform, a sensor capability, or a program someone wants kept off the record. The other possibility is that the unresolved stamp serves a purpose of its own.

Either way, the label became a decision, and somebody made it. Kirkpatrick has now said that the decision came from above his office.

What Every Unresolved Label Is Worth Now

Before Kirkpatrick spoke, an unresolved stamp read as a plain statement: the analysts tried and came up empty.

Now every one of those stamps raises a second question. Was the case never solved, or was it solved and cut?

The public has no way to sort one pile from the other, and the War Department refuses to sort them. Only the people who held the original findings can, and the first of them has gone on camera to say the pile was salted. The longer trail of hidden findings is in the Vault.

Three facts now sit on the record. The first director of AARO says the cases had answers. He says he briefed those answers to Congress. He says Pentagon leadership ordered them removed before the files went public.

That reverses the burden. If the answers were boring, why were they removed? If they were something else, which cases on that list are being protected, and who signed the order? Tell us which file you would pull first.
