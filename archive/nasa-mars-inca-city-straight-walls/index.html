Inca City on Mars: The Walls NASA's Own Camera Team Named

By Jordan Vale · November 22, 2026 · 7 min read

Inca City is a grid of straight ridges that meet at sharp corners near the south pole of Mars. NASA's Mariner 9 first photographed it in 1972, and its scientists named it after the ruins of the Inca. Today the HiRISE camera team at the University of Arizona photographs it from NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter. On its own page, that team says the formation is reminiscent of the ruins of an ancient city and that its origin is not known for certain. The people closest to the photographs looked at the walls and called it a city.

That name has survived for more than fifty years. Since 1972 Mars has been mapped from orbit by three space agencies and at least four spacecraft. Every new camera arrived with sharper eyes, and every one of them flew home with the same picture: straight walls, hard corners, and a grid that holds its shape across miles of open ground.

The Walls Mariner 9 Found in 1972

Mariner 9 reached Mars in November 1971 and became the first spacecraft to orbit another planet. A global dust storm hid the surface for weeks. When the storm cleared in 1972, the cameras began sending back the south polar region, and in those frames sat a pattern that looked built. The team called it Inca City.

The name stuck because the picture matched it. Long, narrow ridges ran in straight lines across the ground. They crossed each other and turned at angles that looked surveyed. The cells they enclosed looked like the footprints of rooms. In 1972 nobody on Earth had seen anything like it on another world, and the people who saw it first reached for the word ruins.

Look at the dates. In 1976 the Viking orbiters photographed the Face on Mars in Cydonia, and a NASA press release joked about it. Inca City came four years earlier, and its name came from the mission's own scientists. The formal name is Angustus Labyrinthus, but the agency's working nickname is still Inca City. What the HiRISE Team Wrote on Its Own Page

HiRISE stands for High Resolution Imaging Science Experiment. The Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter launched in August 2005, reached Mars in March 2006, and has carried the camera ever since. From orbit, HiRISE can pick out objects about the size of a kitchen table. The instrument is run out of the Lunar and Planetary Laboratory at the University of Arizona in Tucson.

When the team published its image of Inca City, it described the ridges as reminiscent of the ruins of an ancient city. It also wrote that their origin is not known for certain. That wording sits on a NASA-funded page, under the camera team's name, beside the highest-resolution photograph of the site any agency has released.

The page gives you three facts. Mariner 9's scientists named it a city. HiRISE's scientists kept the name and repeated the comparison. And after five decades of imaging, the same scientists still say they do not know how the walls got there.

They had every word in the geology dictionary. They looked at the walls and chose "city."

A Grid Inside a Circle Fifty-Three Miles Wide

Inca City sits near 81 degrees south latitude, close to the edge of the south polar cap. Higher-resolution images from NASA's Mars Global Surveyor in 2002 showed that the ridges are part of a much larger ring. The ring is roughly 86 kilometers across, about fifty-three miles, and the grid runs along its inside edge.

That layout matters. A ring with a grid laid inside it is how a planned settlement looks from the air. Earth has examples on several continents: enclosure walls, internal blocks, and straight lanes cutting across them. Lay the Mars image beside an aerial survey of a walled site and the shapes line up. The Rock Explanation, and Why It Breaks

Some planetary geologists say the ridges started as cracks in the ground. Molten rock or mineral-rich fluid filled the cracks and hardened. The softer ground around them then wore away, leaving the hard fill standing as ridges. Geologists call these dikes. Another version starts with sand dunes that turned to stone.

Look at how cracks actually behave. A dike follows whatever fracture it fills, and fractures branch, wander, and taper off. They fan out from a center or spread like a web. Inca City has long straight runs that hold their line and then turn sharply at a corner, again and again, in a repeating grid. Fossil dunes curve, because wind makes curves.

The strongest witness against the rock story is the camera team itself. If dikes settled the question, the HiRISE page would say dikes. It says the origin is not known for certain. Planetary science has spent fifty years studying these walls, and the best word it has found is still "city."

2024: The Spiders Moved In

In 2024 the European Space Agency released new orbital images of the site from its Mars Express orbiter and the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter. They showed hundreds of dark, branching marks spread across the ground between the walls. The ESA team called them spiders.

The spiders appear in the Martian spring. As the sun warms the polar ice, frozen carbon dioxide turns to gas beneath the surface, bursts upward, and throws dark dust across the ground in branching patterns. The walls of Inca City stand in the middle of that activity every year and keep their straight lines.

That is the detail that should stop you. Mars is still reshaping this ground. Ice breaks it apart every spring, wind scours it, and dust buries it. Fifty years of seasons have left the walls straight. Whatever built them built them to last.

What a City on Mars Would Actually Look Like

A city on Mars would have no reason to look like Machu Picchu. It would have no terraces cut for llamas and no stone cut to Andean taste. After an unknown age of Martian weather, a structure would be worn down to its bones. Those bones are foundations, enclosure walls, and the straight lines that minds put on landscapes.

Straight lines are the signature. Rivers, lava, and wind leave curves. Builders leave straight runs and hard corners, and Inca City has both. The scientists who first saw it read that signature in 1972, and the scientists photographing it now repeat their comparison. Five decades, four spacecraft, and three agencies have photographed this grid, and the record comes back the same each time. Mariner 9 found it. NASA's HiRISE team calls it reminiscent of an ancient city. ESA's 2024 images showed its walls standing straight through the Martian spring.

HiRISE can see objects the size of a kitchen table. It has looked at Inca City, and the team behind it still writes that the origin is not known for certain. So the real question goes beyond what the walls are. Why does the camera that can see everything still have no answer for them? Look at the grid and tell us what you see.
