Egypt's King List Shows The Gods Ordered Off The Throne

By Jordan Vale · November 06, 2026 · 7 min read

Egypt's own king list records the gods ruling first and then handing the throne to men. The Turin King List is a papyrus from the reign of Ramesses II, now in the Museo Egizio in Turin, Italy. It opens with gods on the throne, Geb, Osiris, Set, Horus and Thoth among them. Next come the spirits and demigods, and only after them a human king named Menes. The priest Manetho counted more than thirteen thousand years of divine rule before that handover. In December 2020, Haim Eshed, the retired brigadier general who ran Israel's space security program for nearly thirty years, said a Galactic Federation exists and keeps its distance because humanity is not ready. Egypt wrote down the withdrawal. Eshed described the rule behind it.

Put the two records side by side and you get one story. Beings ruled in the open, taught, built and kept the accounts. Then they stepped down from the throne, and a scribe logged the change as a new line in a ledger. A god does not give up a throne by choice. Someone above the gods gave the order.

The Papyrus That Opens On Gods

The Turin King List came out of the ground at Thebes, modern Luxor, and passed through the collection of the French consul Bernardino Drovetti before it reached the royal collection of Sardinia in Turin in the 1820s. It travelled in a box, unprotected, and arrived crumbled into fragments. Jean-François Champollion, the man who cracked hieroglyphs, bent over those pieces in Turin in 1824. The German scholar Gustavus Seyffarth spent years fitting them back together. Today the reassembled sheet sits under glass in the Museo Egizio.

The scribe wrote it in hieratic on the back of a used tax register. That detail matters. The list sits on a sheet that came out of an accounting office. Its first column was written as a record of who held the throne and for how long.

Gods, Then Spirits, Then A Man

The first entries on the papyrus are gods. Ptah and Ra lead the line, then Shu, then Geb, Osiris, Set and Horus, with Thoth and Maat following. Each name sits inside the same royal cartouche later used for pharaohs. Each one gets a reign length.

After the gods comes a block the scribe calls the akhu, the spirits, along with the Shemsu Hor, the Followers of Horus. These are the demigods, a middle rank between the makers and the men. They hold the throne for a span the papyrus measures in thousands of years.

Only then does Menes appear. He is the first human king, the founder of the First Dynasty, the man every chronology since has used as Year One of Egypt. On the papyrus he is a successor. He inherits a throne that was already ancient when he sat on it.

Three Witnesses, One Ledger

The Turin scribe was not alone. Manetho, a priest of Sebennytos in the Nile Delta, wrote the Aegyptiaca for Ptolemy II in the third century BC with temple archives open in front of him. His king lists survive through the chronicler Eusebius and the Byzantine monk George Syncellus. Manetho puts the gods first, counts their rule at roughly 13,900 years, and follows them with demigods and spirits of the dead before Menes.

Two centuries earlier, Herodotus sat with Egyptian priests and wrote down what they told him in Book II of his Histories. Before mortal kings, gods ruled Egypt and lived among men, and the last of them to hold the throne was Horus, son of Osiris. Four centuries after Herodotus, Diodorus Siculus recorded in Book I of his Library of History that gods and heroes ruled Egypt for a little under eighteen thousand years, with Horus again named as the last god-king.

That gives three separate hands across three separate centuries with one order of events. Gods sat on the throne, demigods followed, then men took over. The stories agree because they were all drawn from the same temple records.

The Bookkeeping Problem

The museum labels file this opening column as religious preamble, mythology placed in front of the real kings. That reading falls apart on the papyrus itself. The scribe wrote reign lengths for the gods in the same format he used for Menes and every pharaoh after him: a name, a cartouche, a count of years. Accountants do not mix myth into a column of numbers. The column was there to record how long each one held the office.

The gods got the same cartouche and the same count of years as the pharaohs. Egypt filed its makers under administration.

And the ledger records a handover. The gods give way to spirits, the spirits give way to a man, and there is no battle in between. Nobody records a war or the fall of an empire. The papyrus shows a transfer of office, and an office only changes hands that cleanly when someone with authority orders it.

Egypt held on to the memory of that moment for three thousand years. The temple builders at Edfu called the age of the gods Zep Tepi, the First Time, and carved its story into the temple walls. Every later pharaoh claimed to be Horus on earth, which makes every pharaoh a caretaker for a ruler who had left.

He said they are waiting for us to develop to the point where we can understand.

That is a policy of distance, and it is the same rule written into the Egyptian ledger. Beings with the power to rule chose to hold back, and the choice came from a council. Eshed named the council. Egypt recorded the result three thousand years before he spoke.

The Order Above The Throne

The question the Turin papyrus raises is who outranks a god. Ptah, Ra, Osiris and Horus are written as kings, and a king answers to nobody inside his own kingdom. When Horus hands the throne to the spirits, the order has to come from outside Egypt, from a body Egypt had no word for. The scribes wrote that it happened and left the reason out.
